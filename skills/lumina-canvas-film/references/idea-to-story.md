# Idea to story

Use this guide when the user gives a subject, feeling, fragment, or broad theme without a complete brief.

## Infer, then make a choice

Read the prompt for its strongest signal: a person or place, a want or problem, a striking contrast, an emotion, or a change. Pick one angle that makes that signal visible. A good angle is specific enough to draw and broad enough to support several scenes.

Convert the idea into:

- **Subject:** what or whom the piece follows.
- **Point of view:** whose experience or which detail makes it distinct.
- **Tension or change:** what shifts, is discovered, is remembered, or is resolved.
- **Visual hook:** one image or action the audience can recall.
- **Close:** an ending image that answers, echoes, or deliberately reframes the opening.

Do not merely restate the user's topic. Add a thoughtful angle while keeping their intent recognizable. Prefer a clear, human-scale premise over a broad survey.

## When the prompt is very open

If the user says only “make something beautiful,” infer a gentle, self-contained subject and choose a coherent style; do not demand a topic questionnaire. If the prompt could lead to substantially different works, offer up to three short concepts and identify the strongest recommendation. If the user asked for creation, continue with the recommendation in the same turn.

## Default assumptions

When details are absent, assume a short visual piece for a general audience, 4–7 scenes, no voice-over, and concise labels. Adapt these defaults to the topic and canvas size. Do not invent personal history, named people, or factual claims to fill gaps.

## Keep the user in control

Treat explicit subjects, mood, audience, exclusions, and supplied material as constraints. Do not replace a user's stated idea with a trend or unrelated concept. Make the creative interpretation easy to revise by summarizing it in one sentence before or alongside the canvas work.
